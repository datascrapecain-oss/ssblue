# Phase 6: Final Decision Logic

Every product you analyse must end with a decision. Analysis without a conclusion is just observation, and observation doesn't move a business forward.

By the time a product reaches this phase, it's passed multiple filters. The maths works, demand is understandable, competition is survivable, and there are no obvious hard stops. What remains is judgement. This final phase turns structure into action, without pretending certainty exists.

## Reject: The Default Outcome

Rejection is the most common result of proper analysis, and the healthiest. Most products should not be bought. That's not a weakness of the system. It's proof the system is working. Every product you reject preserves capital, reduces operational noise, and prevents avoidable problems later.

A clean rejection is a successful outcome. There's no requirement to force a buy simply because time was spent analysing. Experienced sellers reject far more products than they ever purchase.

## Park / Monitor: Used Intentionally, Not Emotionally

Parking a product isn't indecision. It's a deliberate choice to wait for change. You might park a product when pricing is temporarily unfavourable, Amazon has recently exited or entered the listing, competition is in flux, or demand is seasonal.

Parking should be intentional and limited. If too many products are parked, the system loses its purpose. A parked product needs a clear reason for being revisited, not vague hope that it'll improve.

## Proceed Cautiously: What a Real "Yes" Looks Like

Proceeding doesn't mean committing heavily, it means testing carefully. Even when a product passes every gate, risk remains, prices move, sellers react, Amazon intervenes. No amount of analysis removes that.

This is why experienced sellers would rather look back and think "I wish I had bought more" than "I wish I had bought less." A cautious entry limits exposure, preserves flexibility, and lets real-world feedback inform the next move. Confidence grows through confirmation, not assumption.

## Why We Never Say "Strong Buy"

There's no such thing as a risk-free product on Amazon. Language like "strong buy" implies certainty that doesn't exist and encourages overcommitment, it replaces judgement with confidence and confidence with complacency.

Passing analysis doesn't mean a product will work. It means it's not obviously bad at the time you checked it. That distinction matters.

## Gates Are a Starting Point, Not a Substitute for Experience

This gate-based framework gives clarity early on, providing clear yes and no decisions while experience is still developing. As you analyse more products, your speed increases, patterns become familiar, and subtle warning signs stand out earlier. You'll rely less on consciously stepping through each gate and more on instinct informed by repetition. That flexibility is earned over time, not shortcut to.

## The Long-Term Goal

The goal isn't perfection. It's consistency. Combining structured gates with cautious decision-making builds pattern recognition, risk awareness, and discipline under uncertainty. Over time, speed and confidence increase naturally, not because risk disappears, but because you understand it better.

Every purchase carries risk. The skill is managing it, not denying it.
